A small dataset, three approaches

Smart MCQ Solver ranks the three most likely answers to a question with five choices, A through E. I compared a recurrent model trained from scratch, a fine-tuned language model, and a retrieval system, then combined their predictions for the competition.

Two snapshots of the project. The August 2026 technical report recorded a public MAP@3 of 0.75519 at rank 293, above the 0.73 cutoff. The updated result supplied for this portfolio is rank 31 of 1,530, with 0.76936 shown in the leaderboard screenshot. The experiments below retain the report's original validation figures.

The aim was to compare different modeling strategies under the same split, prevent related questions from leaking across validation boundaries, and save the models, predictions, and Weights & Biases runs so the experiments could be checked again.

Understanding the question families

SplitRowsFields
Training2,000id, prompt, choices A–E, answer
Test500id, prompt, choices A–E; answers hidden

There were no missing values. The important pattern was repetition: different instruction wrappers often surrounded the same underlying question. After cleaning, the 2,000 training rows reduced to 252 question families; 1,970 rows belonged to repeated families. All 228 test families also appeared in training.

The answer distribution was A: 369, B: 490, C: 459, D: 358, and E: 324. Raw prompts averaged 18.15 words, with a median of 17 and a maximum of 51. Cleaned prompts averaged 11.72 words, with a median of 11.

Cleaning without losing the useful text

I applied Unicode NFKC normalization and removed six common prefixes and four suffixes that acted as instruction wrappers. Two versions of each prompt served different purposes:

  • content_prompt preserved case and punctuation for the neural models.
  • clean_prompt used lowercase text, replaced punctuation and underscores with spaces, and normalized whitespace for grouping and retrieval.

Keeping related questions together

A three-fold StratifiedGroupKFold split with seed 42 grouped rows by canonical question while trying to maintain answer-label balance. Fold 0 contained 1,277 training rows from 167 families and 723 validation rows from 85 families. No family appeared on both sides. I did not generate additional questions because the dataset already contained many natural wrapper variants.

Why this matters: validation tested unseen question families, while the competition test set contained families already present in the full training set. Those are different conditions, and the leaderboard score should be interpreted with that difference in mind.

How the three models work

Each neural model scored five question-and-choice pairs. The retrieval model instead matched the cleaned question against stored families and used their answer letters.

BiGRU: a compact model trained from scratch

The sequence format was [BOS] question [SEP] choice [EOS], with padding masks to distinguish real tokens from padding. A sample batch had shape [32, 5, 142]: 32 questions, five choices, and 142 padded token positions.

A learned embedding converted each token into 128 values. A bidirectional GRU read the sequence in both directions with 128 hidden values per direction. Masked mean and max pooling formed a 512-value representation per choice, followed by 25% dropout and an answer score. All 868,993 parameters were trained from random initialization.

DeBERTa: fine-tuning the last four layers

DeBERTa-v3-base has 12 layers, a hidden size of 768, and 12 attention heads. It separates content and position information in attention. Five question-and-choice pairs produced one score for each of A–E.

I froze the input layers and the first eight transformer layers, training the last four layers and answer head. This left 28,942,849 trainable parameters out of 184,422,913.

Small batches, gradient accumulation, and frozen layers helped fit the model into the available GPU memory. The pretrained tokenizer was retained to keep token IDs consistent with the model.

Retrieval: canonical lookup, then TF-IDF

The CPU-based system stored one cleaned question per training family. It checked for an exact canonical match first; otherwise, character TF-IDF and cosine similarity retrieved nearby questions.

I compared character ranges 4–6 and 5–7 with 30 or 50 neighbours. The best setting used 4–6 characters and 30 neighbours. Similar questions voted for answer letters, with training-label frequencies providing a fallback when similarity was weak. An exact match assigned 90% of the score to the stored answer and 10% to fallback scores.

TF weights repeated character groups, while IDF increases the influence of rarer groups. Cosine similarity compares the resulting vectors. Unlike the neural models, this system has no epoch-based training loop.

Limitation: it matches question text rather than the meaning of the answer choices. Reordering the options for the same question can make a stored answer letter incorrect.

Reading the evaluation metric

With one correct answer per question, MAP@3 is the mean reciprocal rank within the first three predictions. Each submission row must contain three different answer letters in ranked order, such as A C B.

Try the scoring rule


1.0000

A correct first prediction earns 1 point for this question.

The contribution is 1 for first place, 1/2 for second, 1/3 for third, and 0 outside the top three. For validation ID 1, the correct answer was B: BiGRU predicted E B D and earned 0.5; DeBERTa predicted A D E and earned 0.

Accuracy measures how often the first prediction is correct. Macro F1 averages F1 across the five answer letters, giving each label equal weight despite the imbalance. Together, these metrics show different aspects of the same model.

Why the final blend favoured retrieval

The submission used 10% BiGRU, 10% DeBERTa, and 80% retrieval. This blend did not beat BiGRU on grouped validation. It was selected because all 500 test rows matched a family in the full training data, while validation intentionally contained no exact family matches.

Before submission, I checked the ID order, allowed letters, and three unique predictions per row. The report's public score was 0.75519, or 0.02519 above the 0.73 cutoff. The updated portfolio result appears at the top of this page.

The useful distinction: the retrieval-heavy blend fit this competition's repeated-family structure. Its leaderboard result does not establish the same performance on completely new questions.

What I learned and what comes next

A larger pretrained model was not automatically the best choice for this small, repetitive dataset. Understanding the question families was as important as the model architecture, and grouped validation gave a harder but fairer view of generalization.

Early stopping addressed BiGRU memorization. Freezing early layers, FP16, and gradient accumulation addressed DeBERTa memory constraints. Fixed seeds, model checkpoints, saved predictions, and separate experiment runs made the work reproducible.

The report proposes experimenting with larger pretrained models and further fine-tuning. Another clear limitation to address is retrieval's dependence on answer-letter order before using it for broader question answering.
